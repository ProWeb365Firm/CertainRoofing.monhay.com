A fair roof claim begins with documentation, a careful reading of the policy, qualified inspection, and line-by-line comparisons of damage and repair scope. A low first estimate is not always a final decision, and a high contractor estimate is not automatically correct. The goal is to prove the reasonable cost of restoring covered damage under the policy, including required work that may not be visible during the initial visit. This Minnesota guide explains how to protect your position while keeping contractor, adjuster, public adjuster, and attorney roles clear.

Key Takeaways

  • Read your declarations page and endorsements first, focusing on wind or hail deductibles, depreciation, exclusions, deadlines, and roof payment schedules.
  • Photograph all damage, prevent added loss, save receipts, and keep a dated record of every call, email, inspection, and document.
  • Use a licensed Minnesota contractor for construction evidence, but remember contractors cannot interpret coverage or adjust claims without proper licensing.
  • Compare estimates line by line, then support supplements with measurements, photographs, invoices, code requirements, and manufacturer instructions.
  • Challenge errors in writing, request reinspection when justified, and consider Commerce, appraisal, a licensed public adjuster, or legal advice or counsel when needed.

1. Understand What the Policy Promises

A. Start with the declarations and endorsements
Look for:

  • A separate wind or hail deductible, including any percentage deductible.
  • Replacement cost or actual cash value settlement for the roof.
  • A roof-surface payment schedule based on material and age.
  • Cosmetic-damage exclusions for metal or other components.
  • Ordinance or law coverage for code-required upgrades.
  • Matching or limited-matching terms.
  • Deadlines to report, repair, submit proof, or recover depreciation.
  • Duties after loss, including protecting the property and cooperating with inspection.

Ask the insurer to identify the exact policy language behind a coverage decision. Keep the policy version that was active on the storm date.

B. Know the difference between ACV and RCV
Actual cash value, or ACV, reflects the damaged property’s value after depreciation. Replacement cost value, or RCV, showcases the covered cost to repair or replace with comparable property, subject to policy terms and limits.

With replacement cost coverage, the insurer may first pay the allowed replacement amount minus depreciation and the deductible. After completed work and required documentation, it may release eligible recoverable depreciation. Nonrecoverable depreciation, excluded work, upgrades, and costs above approved amounts may remain the owner’s responsibility.

For example, if the approved covered replacement amount is $20,000, depreciation is $5,000, and the deductible is $2,500, an initial payment might be $12,500. The recoverable portion may be paid after completion, depending on the policy. This is an illustration, not a promise of payment.

C. Coverage and price are different disputes
The insurer first determines whether the event is covered. It then outlines the covered scope and amount. Appraisal provisions commonly address the amount of loss, not whether the policy covers the damage.

2. Build a Strong Claim File

A. Document before permanent repairs
Take photographs and video from safe locations. Record the storm date, interior leaks, damaged exterior items, temporary work, and discarded material. Save weather alerts and contractor inspection images.

Create a claim folder containing:

  • Policy and endorsements.
  • Claim number and contact list.
  • Photographs and videos.
  • Emergency invoices and receipts.
  • Insurer estimates and letters.
  • Contractor reports and estimates.
  • Permit and code information.
  • Product instructions and availability information.
  • A dated communication log.

Proper organization makes it easier to find omissions and respond before deadlines.

B. Limit further damage
Cover openings, remove standing water, and dry wet materials when safe. Ask the insurer how emergency services should be documented. Keep damaged items until the adjuster has had time to inspect unless they create a safety or health risk.

C. Follow deadlines exactly
Report the claim as required by the policy and respond to information requests. Minnesota law sets claim-handling standards, but those standards do not erase policy deadlines. An insurer may use a certified notice procedure requiring a completed proof of loss within 60 days.

Use email or the carrier portal to create a written record. When sending documents, label them and ask for confirmation of receipt. If the insurer needs additional time to investigate, request the reason and expected completion date in writing.

3. Get a Construction-Based Inspection

A. Hire a qualified local contractor
Select a Minnesota-licensed residential roofer or building contractor with storm-repair experience. The inspection should cover shingles, ridge caps, flashing, vents, gutters, siding, screens, penetrations, attic conditions, and interior moisture where relevant.

The contractor’s estimate must include measurements, quantities, materials, labor operations, permit, disposal, and supporting photographs. It should separate storm damage from wear, maintenance, and optional upgrades.

B. Respect legal role limits
Minnesota law allows contractors to document construction damage and estimate repairs. It prohibits them from interpreting policy provisions, advising the insured about coverage duties, or adjusting the claim for the insured unless they also hold a public adjuster license.

That distinction protects homeowners from conflicts. A contractor should state, “This flashing must be replaced to perform the repair correctly,” not, “Your policy must cover this.” Coverage questions belong with the insurer, a licensed public adjuster, or legal counsel.

C. Avoid illegal inducements
Minnesota contractors cannot offer to pay, waive, absorb, or rebate the insurance deductible. They also cannot use rewards, free upgrades, referral payments, meals, or yard-sign compensation to induce insurance-funded work. The homeowner must pay the deductible.

Demand an itemized, good-faith estimate with any agreement authorizing repairs. Never sign a blank scope or a document described vaguely as permission to “work with insurance.” Read assignment, cancellation, payment, and fee terms first.

4. Compare Estimates Line by Line

A. Do not compare only the bottom number
Two estimates can differ by thousands of dollars while using similar unit prices because one leaves out operations. Compare roof measurements and every line item.

Common points of difference include:

  • Number of layers to remove.
  • Roof area, waste, ridge, hip, valley, and starter quantities.
  • Ice barrier and underlayment.
  • Drip edge and roof-to-wall flashing.
  • Chimney, skylight, plumbing vent, and mechanical flashing.
  • Ventilation changes required during reroofing.
  • Steep or high-roof labor.
  • Decking replacement.
  • Detaching and resetting gutters or accessories.
  • Permit, disposal, taxes, and interior protection.

B. Use evidence for supplements
A supplement is a documented request to add or revise scope after the first estimate. It is common because some conditions are hidden until tear-off. The contractor should send clear support, not a higher total.

Useful support includes:

  • Labeled photographs and measurements.
  • Product installation instructions.
  • Written local building department guidance.
  • Permit and inspection requirements.
  • Supplier invoices or product unavailability records.
  • Evidence of extra layers or damaged decking.
  • A revised itemized estimate explaining each change.

When hidden damage appears, pause long enough to document it and notify the insurer when the policy or carrier process requires inspection.

C. Handle code items accurately
Minnesota replacement coverage can include the cost to comply with minimum code required by state or local authorities, subject to policy limits and the coverage that applies. In a partial loss, coverage may be limited to the damaged portion unless the policy provides more.

Do not call every best practice a code requirement. Cite the adopted rule, local interpretation, manufacturer instruction, or permit correction that makes the item necessary. For Twin Cities sloped roofs, ice barrier, underlayment, flashing, sheathing, and ventilation details frequently deserve careful review.

5. Push Back on Errors Professionally

A. Ask for the basis in writing
If the estimate omits a damaged slope or necessary operation, send a short written request. Identify the disputed item, explain the construction reason, attach proof, and ask for a written response. Avoid emotional claims that the insurer is lowballing before checking measurements and scope.

Minnesota claim-handling law generally requires insurers to acknowledge a claim and respond to communications within stated business-day periods, with exceptions and details in the statute. Keep dates. A clear paper trail helps if the issue is escalated.

B. Request reinspection when facts justify it
A reinspection is useful when the first visit missed inaccessible slopes, occurred in poor conditions, used incorrect measurements, or overlooked documented collateral damage. Ask the contractor to attend and discuss construction facts.

Before the meeting, prepare a one-page list of disputed items. During the inspection, stay factual. Afterward, send a written recap and request the revised estimate or denial reasoning.

C. Use escalation tools in the right order
If the claim remains unresolved, options may include:

  • A supervisor or internal claim review.
  • A Minnesota Department of Commerce inquiry or complaint.
  • The appraisal process stated in the policy for an amount-of-loss dispute.
  • A licensed public adjuster who represents the insured for a fee.
  • An attorney experienced in Minnesota property insurance when legal rights or coverage are disputed.

A public adjuster is optional, not required. Compare licenses, disciplinary history, contracts, percentages, services, and likely benefit before hiring one.

6. Protect Yourself Through Construction and Payment

A. Understand the contract before signing
The written contract should describe materials, scope, price calculation, payment schedule, timeline, permits, cleanup, warranties, and change-order process. Avoid contracts having an undefined insurance-proceeds price and no itemized good-faith estimate.

Minnesota provides a right to cancel certain insurance-funded residential roofing contracts within 72 hours after notice that the insurer denied the claim. The contract should contain the required notice. Other cancellation rights may apply based on how the sale occurred.

B. Coordinate mortgage payments
If a mortgage company is named on an insurance check, inquire early about endorsement, inspections, and draw procedures. Large claims may be released in stages. Do not promise payment dates to the contractor until the lender process is understood.

Track insurer payments by category including ACV, supplements, recoverable depreciation, and other structures or interior work. Insurance proceeds are not extra spending money when unfinished covered repairs remain.

C. Close the claim with records
After work, obtain the final invoice, proof of payment, permit sign-off, warranties, completion photos, and any requested certificate. Submit required documents for recoverable depreciation before the policy deadline.

Compare final approved scope with actual work. If an item was paid but not completed, resolve it transparently with the insurer and contractor. Never approve an inflated invoice or false completion statement. Those actions can create coverage, legal, and fraud problems.

FAQs

1. Can my insurer require its preferred roofing contractor?

An insurer may recommend vendors, but homeowners generally should ask whether they may choose their own qualified contractor. Coverage and reasonable cost still depend on the policy. Get selection rights and warranty responsibilities in writing.

2. What is a roof supplement?

It is a supported request to revise the insurer’s scope or amount when necessary work, quantities, pricing, or hidden conditions were not included. This is not automatic extra money. Documentation and policy coverage are still controlled.

3. Is appraisal the same as hiring a public adjuster?

No. Appraisal is a policy process for resolving amount-of-loss disagreements through appraisers and, when needed, an umpire. A public adjuster represents the policyholder during claim preparation or negotiation for a fee.

4. Can I keep recoverable depreciation without replacing the roof?

Usually it is released only after covered repair or replacement and required proof, but policy terms control. Ask the insurer about deadlines and acceptable documentation. Never submit false invoices or completion statements.

5. What if matching shingles are discontinued?

Document the exact product, repair area, availability, and whether a repair can meet installation and appearance requirements. Policy wording and Minnesota law affect the answer. Request a written coverage decision rather than assuming full replacement is guaranteed.
